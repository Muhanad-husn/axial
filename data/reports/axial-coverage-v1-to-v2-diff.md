**Thirty-one books to thirty-five, measured**

6 August 2026 · companion to [axial-coverage-v2.md](axial-coverage-v2.md)

---

## Read the baseline number first

**Version 1.0 reported 49,674 pages for thirty-one books. Recounting the same
thirty-one books out of today's index gives 43,874 — 11.7% fewer.**

Nothing was lost from the corpus. The index was rebuilt between the two reports,
and the rebuild carried the transliteration fold (#642) and the merge changes in
#677, both of which collapse names that were separate pages on 2 August. Works
absorbed most of it: 8,583 pages then, 6,097 for the same books now, a 29% drop
that is what better matching of book titles looks like.

That number is not separately attributed to a single change, and this note does
not try to. It matters only because it rules out the obvious comparison. **Every
figure below compares the thirty-one books as counted today against all
thirty-five as counted today**, so the difference is the four books and nothing
else. Comparing against the printed version 1.0 tables would credit the four
books with a 5,800-page loss they had no part in.

The shape did not move. Two in three pages are still mentioned once; 83% still
sit inside a single book. Adding 11% more material bought 7.5% more usable pages
and left every proportion where it was. That is the expected result of adding
books at this scale, and it is worth stating plainly: **a four-book addition
changes which pages are good, not what the library looks like.**

## How much of it landed on the shelf

The four books touch 5,662 pages between them. **1,952 of those pages — 34.5% —
already existed at thirty-one books.** The other 3,710 are new, and 2,609 of
those are people, institutions and works that no other book on the shelf
mentions.

Counted by mention rather than by page, the picture is better, because the
overlap is where the density is:

| Book | Pages touched | Already on the shelf | Share of its mentions landing on an existing page |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Hinnebusch 1990 | 1,517 | 678 (44.7%) | **67.4%** |
| Gelvin 1998 | 2,001 | 699 (34.9%) | 55.5% |
| Wedeen 2019 | 1,823 | 618 (33.9%) | 49.0% |
| Gould 2003 | 658 | 275 (41.8%) | 45.5% |

Hinnebusch is the model addition: two-thirds of what it says lands on a page
another author is already talking on. Gould is the weakest — smallest footprint,
fewest mentions on shared ground — which is what a theory book with a different
case base looks like against this shelf.

## The verdict

The four books did what three Syria case studies would be expected to do. They
deepened the library's strongest region, converted forty single-voice pages into
argued ones, and added twenty-three net pages to the research band. They did not
touch a single one of the gaps version 1.0 identified, and they pushed the
shelf's six best Syria and Mandate pages past the size a reader can use.

**The next addition should not be about Syria.** The sovereignty gap, the missing
critic of Mann and the unanswered Chouliaraki and Agamben are all exactly where
they were, and are now a larger share of what is wrong with the shelf than they
were four books ago.
