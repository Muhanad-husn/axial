# Comparative: nationalism and nation-state formation, nineteenth and twentieth centuries

- **[stated]** The Palestinian case demonstrates the limits of bellicist logic: when external alliances supply strategic rent that obviates the need for domestic extraction, internal struggles over resource extraction are marginalized, state-society relations remain unresolved, and war can undermine rather than build the state, producing a fragile, fragmented postcolonial polity. - **[stated]** Mann argues that the rise of the modern nation-state with citizenship, nationalism, and sacred geopolitical interests reinforced realist presuppositions, making statesmen more likely to behave in realist ways, and that larger territorial states infused with popular nationalism created more military-political definitions of national interest. # Comparative: quasi-states, recognition and international statebuilding, 1945-2012

**The question:** How do later accounts of sovereignty, recognition and international statebuilding modify Jackson's concept of quasi-states: do they narrow it, extend it, or contest what it was meant to explain?

## Answer

- **[stated]** Jackson argues that the international normative framework of negative sovereignty upholding quasi-states is more likely to persist unchanged than to be transformed by human rights concerns or international pressure, and that sovereign rights take legal priority over human rights in the current international order [c23, c25].   > [passage: 578 words, 1 lines]
- **[stated]** Zaum argues that since the end of the Cold War, sovereignty has changed from the formal-legal entitlement Jackson called 'negative sovereignty' to a condition entailing dual responsibility of the state towards other states and its own citizens, and that international statebuilding reflects this changed understanding [c5]. (Zaum 2007)

  > [passage: 540 words, 1 lines]
- **[stated]** Zaum further argues that recognition of sovereignty, unlike the declaratory recognition of statehood, is an ongoing process that can be guided by norms of empirical statehood and domestic responsibility, meaning a state's domestic authority and capacity now matter for its full sovereign standing—a development Jackson's framework did not anticipate [c14]. (Zaum 2007)

  > [passage: 804 words, 1 lines]
- **[stated]** Caspersen argues that unrecognized states and quasi-states are polar opposites: quasi-states enjoy external sovereignty without internal sovereignty, while unrecognized states survive without external sovereignty but claim internal sovereignty, thereby identifying a reverse disconnect that Jackson's framework did not explore [c6, c9].   > [passage: 1333 words, 11 lines]
- **[stated]** Ayubi argues that post-colonial states are not simply juridical quasi-states but are also 'lopsided'—underdeveloped in certain respects but overdeveloped in others, particularly in their oversized military-bureaucratic apparatus—and that this exaggerated state role can be related to delayed capitalist development (étatisme), requiring political-economy analysis alongside Jackson's juridical framework [c1]. - **[stated]** Jackson himself acknowledges political-economy pressures on quasi-states, arguing that IMF restructuring programmes constitute 'classical positive sovereignty doctrine' that requires incompetent governments to conform to banking disciplines, and that the coexistence of substantial states and quasi-states creates a constitutional dilemma in international economic relations between free trade and redistribution [c27, c29]. ## The opposing position

Fragmented Syria is better understood as several empirical polities pursuing incremental secession than as one juridical sovereign under stress. The primary claim's rejection of AANES/Idlib as Caspersen-style de facto states because they lacked 'an explicit declaration of independence' directly misapplies Caspersen's own framework: Caspersen explicitly states that a formal declaration of independence is 'not necessarily decisive' and identifies 'incremental secession' cases—entities that 'have not formally declared independence yet function as independent entities and display aspirations for independence'—as qualifying for de facto statehood. By Caspersen's own criteria, the absence of a formal declaration does not exclude the AANES or Idlib entities. The corpus further provides evidence of genuine empirical institution-building that the Jackson frame obscures: alternative property registries and legal systems operated by local councils and the Free Syrian Lawyers Association in opposition-held areas (vignal-2021), a 'delicate architecture of power' with militias and non-state actors providing varying levels of governance across Idlib, Kurdish areas, Aleppo, and Homs (kao-2025), and Malešević's argument that civil wars can produce state formation rather than merely destroy it. The regime's juridical recognition, sustained not by international norms of non-intervention alone but by active great-power intervention (kandiah-2018), does not negate the empirical reality of multiple functioning polities. Collapsing the analysis into a Jacksonian quasi-state framework obscures the genuine, if externally constrained, empirical statehood that developed in Syria's fragmented regions. (Caspersen 2012; Kao 2025; Malešević 2026; Vignal 2021; Kandiah 2018)
